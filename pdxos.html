The Data Was There.
Nobody Was Looking at It.

How a fresh perspective, a rigorous SWOT analysis, and three deliberate product strategies turned an 89% revenue collapse into a 506% recovery — for a beloved Portland nonprofit arts organization.

The Situation

A 20-year-old organization running on assumptions it had never tested

Portland Open Studios had been Portland's premier open studio event for two decades. Artists applied, a jury selected approximately 100 participants, and every October patrons toured studios across the city. The organization had a 20-year brand, a loyal audience, and a volunteer board that cared deeply about the mission.

It also had a product problem it had been ignoring for years.

Admission to the event was sold through two printed items: a Tour Guide (structured as a calendar) and a separate map. In 2010, a mobile app had been built — but the board was afraid to promote it, worried it would cannibalize Tour Guide sales. So they didn't. Meanwhile, the Tour Guide went through a series of format experiments — 8.5x11, then 8x10, then a catastrophic 17x11 vertical calendar in 2014 — and sales collapsed from 768 units in 2011 to just 84 in 2014.

Kerri attended the event in 2013 as a regular patron. Her experience was chaotic. A few months later, she attended a board meeting to share feedback — and discovered an organization in quiet crisis, bound by fear, tradition, and a product strategy that the data had been disproving for years.

"It is not simply the availability of the mobile app that is causing the decrease in tour guide sales. It is the decreasing demand for a printed calendar product."

The Detective's Eye

The board had framed the problem as a technology threat — the app was stealing sales. The data told a different story: the product itself had stopped working. The calendar format was declining across the industry. The map looked free. The organization needed a product strategy, not a technology decision.

Annual surveys, point-of-sale data, and partner feedback were all available. The picture they painted was unambiguous — once someone was willing to look at it as a whole.

The Strategy

Three decisions that changed everything

Rather than treat the symptoms, Kerri went back to the data and developed a product strategy grounded in what the evidence actually showed. Annual SWOT analysis informed each year's decisions. Three core strategies drove the turnaround.

01

Redesign the Tour Guide format

The 17x11 calendar was a catastrophic failure — frustrating patrons and retail partners alike (the map was being treated as a free product at point-of-sale). Kerri redesigned the Tour Guide into a portable, usable format that gave more space to artists, was easier to carry through the event, and gave graphic designers a consistent standard to work with. The format was locked for the remaining years. Guide sales recovered from 84 units to 509 in the first year — a 506% increase.

02

Eliminate the map-only option

The separate map had been treated as a free flyer at retail — resulting in massive shrinkage and lost revenue. Rather than try to fix a product that patrons didn't perceive as valuable, Kerri eliminated it entirely. This reduced production costs, simplified the product line, and pushed patrons toward either the Tour Guide or the app — both of which generated real revenue and a better patron experience.

03

Stop hiding the app. Make it the low-cost entry point.

For four years the board had been afraid to promote the app, worried it would cut into Tour Guide sales. The data showed the opposite: the app had been quietly growing through word of mouth, and there was an entirely untapped audience for it. Kerri's strategy: reduce the paid app price from $9.99 to $4.99, introduce a free version as the replacement for the eliminated map-only option, and actively promote it. The result: paid app units jumped from 117 in 2014 to 273 in 2015. The free app reached 1,053 downloads in 2017 — an audience the organization had never previously reached. A geo-fencing location marketing campaign in 2017 pushed free app adoption further, targeting patrons in the event footprint in real time. Donors were recruited specifically to underwrite the revenue shift: TicketsWest in 2015, a private patron in 2016, Standard Insurance as a general sponsor in 2017.

The Org Work

Strategy only works if the organization can execute it

Product strategy was only half the challenge. When Kerri joined, the volunteer board was thin — several members at the end of their three-year terms, limited digital expertise, and no one with the capacity to build or maintain the app that had first drawn Kerri in. (The programmer who had built it had joined Amazon and could no longer maintain the code.)

Kerri used the natural board turnover as a strategic opportunity. Every prospective board member was screened for comfort with digital strategy. She wasn't filling seats — she was reshaping the board's collective capability.

An artist came forward with an unusual combination: a full online teaching presence, a journalism background, and genuine readiness to tackle social media. Kerri brought her in as VP. Together they built platform-specific social media strategies, blog content, and press relations. The new VP also recruited additional board members with business experience — growing the active board from 3 members to 11.

Key Pattern

Kerri didn't wait for the perfect hire. She found the person who was already doing the work — the artist who had built an online presence independently — and gave her a role with real authority. The right structure enabled the right person to do more than either could have done alone.

This is the Creative Perspective Detective at work.

Kerri walked into Portland Open Studios as a patron first — someone who experienced the product the way the audience did, without the assumptions that had accumulated over 20 years. That outsider perspective, combined with a willingness to let the data lead, is the methodology. Investigate what's actually happening. Synthesize what the evidence shows. Implement with the organization's real capacity in mind.

In Kerri's words
"Most change happens when an organization is in turmoil. I didn't wait for the right moment. The crisis was the moment."
The Outcome

A sustainable organization with a product strategy built to last

By the time Kerri passed the presidency to her successor in December 2017, Portland Open Studios had a redesigned product line, a functioning digital strategy, a rebuilt board, and three years of documented SWOT analysis and audience data to guide future decisions. The organization that had sold 84 Tour Guides in 2014 sold 453 in 2017 — while also generating 1,053 free app downloads and building an entirely new audience segment.

What this engagement delivered

  • Tour Guide redesigned from a 17x11 failing calendar to a portable, standardized format — 506% unit sales recovery in year one (84 → 509)
  • Map-only product eliminated, reducing production costs and redirecting patrons to higher-value options
  • App strategy overhauled: free tier introduced, paid tier repriced, actively promoted for the first time — free downloads reached 1,053 in 2017
  • Geo-fencing location marketing campaign deployed in 2017 to reach patrons in real time during the event
  • Three-year donor/sponsor recruitment strategy: TicketsWest (2015), private patron (2016), Standard Insurance (2017)
  • Volunteer board rebuilt from 3 active members to 11, with deliberate screening for digital strategy capability
  • VP recruited with journalism, social media, and online teaching expertise — built full multi-platform digital marketing strategy
  • Annual SWOT analysis process established as a decision-making framework, with survey data from artists and visitors informing each year's strategy
  • Complete website redesign (2016–2017) bringing the organization into a coherent digital presence
