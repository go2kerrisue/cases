Building the Operational Infrastructure Behind a National Dementia Care Certification Program

A founder's methodology is only scalable if the operational system behind it actually works. This is the story of building that system — from five disconnected platforms and duplicate data, to a single source of truth supporting 30 organizations and 358 learners in year one.

The Organization

A Founder's Methodology. A Team of Caregivers. Five Disconnected Systems.

Positive Approach to Care (PAC) is built around the dementia care methodology of founder Teepa Snow — one of the most recognized dementia care educators in the world. Her framework, grounded in the GEMS® states and the Six Pieces of the Puzzle, teaches care partners to meet people exactly where they are — honoring their real-time cognitive state, their perspective, and their capacity in any given moment.

It is a profound methodology. And like many founder-led organizations built on a powerful personal philosophy, the operational infrastructure behind it had not kept pace with the vision.

PAC hires care partners — people with deep human skill, empathy, and expertise in dementia care. They are not hired to run business systems. Nobody on the team was. And as the organization began scaling its private corporate certification program, the cracks were showing: a CRM being used for things it wasn't designed for, spreadsheets that some people used and others avoided entirely, and five platforms that didn't talk to each other.

The Origin

I Didn't Arrive as a Consultant. I Came In to Help Someone with Excel.

That's important context. I arrived just before the pandemic, initially to help the e-commerce manager — because not many people on the team understood Excel. PAC tends to hire care partners, not technology people. So one of the dynamics that defined my entire time there was being a systems and technology person in a team of care partners who would take on the tasks they felt they had the ability to do.

Business and technology skills varied widely across the team — as they do in most mission-driven organizations where the hiring priority is rightly the core work. Which meant every systems problem I encountered was also a human problem — and every solution had to account for both.

My first job was to learn the system the way it actually worked — not the way anyone wished it worked. With no manual, no single person who had the full picture, and no clear answers, I documented what existed, asked questions, and built understanding from the ground up alongside the people living in it every day.

Then the pandemic hit.

The Crucible

Change Happens Most When an Organization Is in Turmoil.

When the entire world goes into turmoil, organizations change — fast, and not always cleanly. For PAC, a dementia care organization built on in-person human connection, the pandemic meant reinventing its entire delivery model overnight. Webinars replaced workshops. Virtual certification replaced in-person events. Every system that had been held together with institutional knowledge and personal relationships was suddenly under pressure.

I operated in that environment for years. Building while the ground was shifting. Documenting processes that kept changing. Supporting a team that was simultaneously learning new tools, serving clients in crisis, and trying to keep a founder's methodology intact across a fully virtual world.

"Turmoil is just change without a container. My job is to build the container."

At the end of the pandemic, as the organization stabilized, I was let go. The acute crisis was over. The systems I'd built had done their job.

The Return

They Brought Me Back. As a Consultant This Time.

It wasn't a long gap — only a few months between being let go as an employee and being brought back as a freelancer. And honestly, the groundwork for that return had been laid from the very beginning.

My direct manager Keith had been absorbing the impact of massive, unplanned software changes driven by leadership decisions — often without operational input. My friend Shelly, a fellow systems thinker, had introduced me to Keith in the first place because she knew I had systems and Excel experience and she recognized what the team needed. Both Keith and Shelly stayed at PAC, kept advocating for better infrastructure, and were instrumental in bringing me back.

Right as I returned, PAC hired Jami — someone who immediately saw the massive untapped potential in the private sales channel. The timing was significant. I focused on building the underlying structure and reporting first. Once that foundation existed and the data was visible, Jami had what she needed to make the case to leadership. She used that evidence to gain influence and push for staff role changes that formalized the private certification program as a real business function.

That's how it actually works. The systems work didn't just organize operations — it created the conditions for someone else to lead organizational change. The container made the next move possible.

Key Principle

Finding your allies inside an organization is not a soft skill. It is a core systems competency. No operational infrastructure survives without the people who believe in it, who protect it when leadership pivots, and who know where the bodies are buried when you're trying to understand why something works the way it does. In this case, those people were already there — I just had to recognize them and build alongside them.

The Approach

Define the Problem Before You Build the Solution.

The first thing I do in any systems project is figure out how I can report on what's happening. Not because I love reports. Because if you can't see what's happening, you can't fix it — and you definitely can't scale it.

There's a principle I return to constantly: stop tying yourself to what the solution looks like and start building the theoretical problem. Define the problem down to every last detail. The answer lives inside the definition.

In this case, the problem had three completely distinct dimensions depending on who was looking at it:

Sales & Outreach

Needed pipeline visibility, client intake tracking, and project status at a glance. Currently guarding information in personal spreadsheets.

Org Managers

Needed to know which employees were registered, where they were in the certification pathway, and how to report completions to their leadership.

Individual Learners

Often had no idea the administrative structure existed. Needed the right content, the right Zoom link, the right schedule — at the right time.

Three different user types. Three completely different definitions of success. One system that had to serve all of them simultaneously.

"Stop tying yourself to what the solution is and start to build the theoretical problem. Define the problem down to every last detail. The answer is in the definition."

Where Two Methodologies Meet

Teepa's Philosophy and Operational Systems Thinking Converge.

Here's where the work gets interesting. Teepa teaches care partners to meet people where they are — to honor their real-time cognitive state, their current capacity, their perspective in this moment. In dementia care, this is non-negotiable. A person cannot choose their GEMS® state. You adapt to them.

In organizational development, the same instinct applies — but with an important distinction. People in organizations have the ability and responsibility to adapt as their environment improves for the good of others. Meeting people where they are doesn't mean leaving them there. It means designing the path forward so that moving is easier than staying stuck.

That philosophy shaped every decision in the build — including the most pragmatic one of all.

The Excel Decision

I chose Excel deliberately — and strategically. PAC already knew Insightly was being sunset as their CRM. What they didn't have yet was a committed replacement. I was pushing hard for HubSpot based on research: it was the most obvious choice with the greatest potential to solve a range of their issues simultaneously. But leadership was still evaluating options, and Shelly was doing extensive cleanup work on the existing Insightly data. The last thing any of us wanted was building a new workflow into a system we knew was going away.

Excel solved the immediate problem — something that worked with every system on the market, locked down with heavy dropdown menus and protected fields so it felt like a form rather than a spreadsheet. For a team with varied technology comfort levels, that distinction was everything.

But it served a second purpose too. The Excel template became a working prototype for how the workflows would eventually need to be built in HubSpot — letting Jami and Shelly see the logic before the platform decision was finalized. When PAC eventually did implement HubSpot, the underlying workflow thinking was already done.

I recommended HubSpot before I'd ever used it. They implemented it after I left. I'm still a little jealous I didn't get to see that through — but that's how good systems work sometimes ends: someone else gets to run in the building you helped design.

The Deeper Lesson

The Most Important Systems Hold When Everything Is Falling Apart.

The most important systems are not the ones that work when everything is going well. They are the ones that hold when the organization is in turmoil — when people are scared, when tools are changing, when the founder's vision is outpacing the infrastructure behind it.

That is where this work was done. And that is where the methodology was tested and proven.

Investigate the real problem — not the presenting problem, but the operational and human dynamics underneath it. Synthesize what you find into a design that serves every user type simultaneously. Implement in a way that meets people where they are while moving the whole system forward.

"If you cannot show me the data, how do we build the way to see it? That question is where every system I've ever built actually started."
