From Chaos to Scalable:
Building the Operational Infrastructure Behind a National Dementia Care Certification Program

A founder's methodology is only scalable if the operational system behind it actually works. This is the story of building that system — from five disconnected platforms and duplicate data, to a single source of truth supporting 30 organizations and 358 learners in year one.

The Organization

A Founder's Methodology. A Team of Caregivers. Five Disconnected Systems.

Positive Approach to Care (PAC) is built around the dementia care methodology of founder Teepa Snow — one of the most recognized dementia care educators in the world. Her framework, grounded in the GEMS® states and the Six Pieces of the Puzzle, teaches caregivers to meet people exactly where they are — honoring their real-time cognitive state, their perspective, and their capacity in any given moment.

It is a profound methodology. And like many founder-led organizations built on a powerful personal philosophy, the operational infrastructure behind it had not kept pace with the vision.

PAC hires caregivers — people with deep human skill, empathy, and expertise in dementia care. They are not hired to run business systems. Nobody on the team was. And as the organization began scaling its private corporate certification program, the cracks were showing: a CRM being used for things it wasn't designed for, spreadsheets that some people used and others avoided entirely, and five platforms that didn't talk to each other.

The Origin

I Didn't Arrive as a Consultant. I Came In to Help Someone with Excel.

That's important context. I arrived just before the pandemic, initially to help the e-commerce manager — because not many people on the team understood Excel. PAC tends to hire caregivers, not technology people. So one of the dynamics that defined my entire time there was being a systems and technology person in a team of caregivers who would take on the tasks they felt they had the ability to do.

Nobody was hired based on their ability to run a business. They were hired based on their ability to care for people. Which meant every systems problem I encountered was also a human problem — and every solution had to account for both.

My first job was to learn the system the way it actually worked — not the way anyone wished it worked. With no manual, no single person who had the full picture, and no clear answers, I documented what existed, asked questions, and built understanding from the ground up alongside the people living in it every day.

Then the pandemic hit.

The Crucible

Change Happens Most When an Organization Is in Turmoil.

When the entire world goes into turmoil, organizations change — fast, and not always cleanly. For PAC, a dementia care organization built on in-person human connection, the pandemic meant reinventing its entire delivery model overnight. Webinars replaced workshops. Virtual certification replaced in-person events. Every system that had been held together with institutional knowledge and personal relationships was suddenly under pressure.

I operated in that environment for years. Building while the ground was shifting. Documenting processes that kept changing. Supporting a team that was simultaneously learning new tools, serving clients in crisis, and trying to keep a founder's methodology intact across a fully virtual world.

"Change happens most completely when an organization is in turmoil. Stability is actually the enemy of transformation. The pandemic created the conditions for change that might have taken a decade otherwise."

At the end of the pandemic, as the organization stabilized, I was let go. The acute crisis was over. The systems I'd built had done their job.

The Return

They Brought Me Back. As a Consultant This Time.

Something had shifted in the organization's leadership during those years. They had been through enough — enough chaos, enough improvisation, enough "we don't have a system for this" — to finally understand that they needed a systems person. Not just someone who could use Excel. Someone who could see the whole operational picture and build something that would hold.

That clarity changed everything. This time I had the right stakeholders, the right mandate, and a specific problem to solve: build the administrative infrastructure for PAC's private corporate certification program at scale, consistently, across multiple clients simultaneously.

One of the first things I did was find my allies. Inside PAC there were a small number of people who had been struggling with the same problems long before I arrived — trying to bring systems thinking into an organization that hadn't been built for it, watching leadership make sweeping software decisions without operational input, absorbing the chaos of a platform migration decided on a whim. These were the people who had been fighting in the mud for years. They understood the problems at a depth I couldn't have reached without them.

Key Principle

Finding your allies inside an organization is not a soft skill. It is a core systems competency. No operational infrastructure survives without the people who believe in it, who protect it when leadership pivots, and who know where the bodies are buried when you're trying to understand why something works the way it does.

The Approach

Define the Problem Before You Build the Solution.

The first thing I do in any systems project is figure out how I can report on what's happening. Not because I love reports. Because if you can't see what's happening, you can't fix it — and you definitely can't scale it.

There's a principle I return to constantly: stop tying yourself to what the solution looks like and start building the theoretical problem. Define the problem down to every last detail. The answer lives inside the definition.

In this case, the problem had three completely distinct dimensions depending on who was looking at it:

Sales & Outreach

Needed pipeline visibility, client intake tracking, and project status at a glance. Currently guarding information in personal spreadsheets.

Org Managers

Needed to know which employees were registered, where they were in the certification pathway, and how to report completions to their leadership.

Individual Learners

Often had no idea the administrative structure existed. Needed the right content, the right Zoom link, the right schedule — at the right time.

Three different user types. Three completely different definitions of success. One system that had to serve all of them simultaneously.

"Stop tying yourself to what the solution is and start to build the theoretical problem. Define the problem down to every last detail. The answer is in the definition."

Where Two Methodologies Meet

Teepa's Philosophy and Operational Systems Thinking Converge.

Here's where the work gets interesting. Teepa teaches caregivers to meet people where they are — to honor their real-time cognitive state, their current capacity, their perspective in this moment. In dementia care, this is non-negotiable. A person cannot choose their GEMS® state. You adapt to them.

In organizational development, the same instinct applies — but with an important distinction. People in organizations have the ability and responsibility to adapt as their environment improves for the good of others. Meeting people where they are doesn't mean leaving them there. It means designing the path forward so that moving is easier than staying stuck.

That philosophy shaped every decision in the build — including the most pragmatic one of all.

The Excel Decision

I chose Excel — not because it was the most sophisticated option, but because it worked with every system on the market and because it was the most familiar starting point for a team that found technology intimidating. Then I locked it down: heavy dropdown menus, protected fields, a template that felt like a form rather than a spreadsheet. The message became: this is not a place to figure things out. This is a place to fill things in. For a team of caregivers who had been afraid of Excel, that distinction was everything.

The Deeper Lesson

The Most Important Systems Hold When Everything Is Falling Apart.

The most important systems are not the ones that work when everything is going well. They are the ones that hold when the organization is in turmoil — when people are scared, when tools are changing, when the founder's vision is outpacing the infrastructure behind it.

That is where this work was done. And that is where the methodology was tested and proven.

Investigate the real problem — not the presenting problem, but the operational and human dynamics underneath it. Synthesize what you find into a design that serves every user type simultaneously. Implement in a way that meets people where they are while moving the whole system forward.

"If you cannot show me the data, how do we build the way to see it? That question is where every system I've ever built actually started."
